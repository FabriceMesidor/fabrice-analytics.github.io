01

The Problem

Every year, TheGuarantors' leadership needed a forward-looking deal volume forecast to drive headcount planning, underwriting capacity decisions, and sales targets. Before this project, those numbers were produced by hand — a senior leader would look at prior year actuals, apply a gut-feel growth percentage, and call it a plan.

The problem wasn't just accuracy. It was credibility and defensibility. When actuals diverged from plan — as they always did — there was no model to interrogate, no assumptions to revisit, and no structured way to update the forecast mid-year. Finance would ask why the number was wrong and there was no rigorous answer.

The added complexity: deal volume at TheGuarantors doesn't move in isolation. We operate in the rental housing market, which means our pipeline is directly sensitive to mortgage rates, housing supply, and rental market conditions. A purely statistical model that ignored those signals would miss the most predictive information available.

"The goal wasn't just a number — it was a defensible, updatable model that leadership could present to investors and use to drive real resource decisions."

02

Forecasting Approach

The project ran in three phases: clean and prepare the historical deal series, run a structured model selection process using backtesting, then adjust the winning model's output using macro indicators and business domain judgment.

01
Historical Data Preparation
Pulled 3+ years of monthly deal submission data from Databricks using dbt-modeled mart tables. Handled missing months, corrected for a known data migration gap in 2021, and decomposed the series to confirm trend, seasonality, and residual components before touching any model code.
02
Model Candidate Definition
Used ACF/PACF plots and ADF stationarity tests to narrow the plausible ARIMA order space. Domain knowledge further constrained choices: deal volume has a clear annual seasonal pattern tied to lease renewal cycles, which guided the seasonal differencing decision. Tested six candidate models.
03
Backtesting & Model Selection
Walk-forward backtesting across the last 12 months of history: train on everything before month N, forecast month N, advance by one month, repeat. Evaluated each candidate on MAE, RMSE, and directional accuracy. Selected the model with the best balance of error metrics and interpretability.
04
Macro Adjustment Layer
Applied a structured overlay using three housing market indicators to shift the base ARIMA forecast up or down. Each indicator was normalized, weighted by its historical predictive correlation with deal volume, and combined into a single adjustment multiplier applied monthly.
05
Scenario Planning & Delivery
Produced three scenarios — base, downside, upside — varying the macro adjustment weights. Delivered as a Python-generated report fed into a Power BI dashboard, with monthly actuals tracking against forecast automatically as new data landed in dbt.

SARIMA(2,1,1)(1,1,0)[12] won on every metric — best MAE, best RMSE, and tied for best directional accuracy. But the choice wasn't purely mechanical. The seasonal component (period=12) aligned with what the business already understood: deal volume has a strong annual rhythm driven by lease renewal cycles peaking in summer and troughing in January. A non-seasonal model would have needed the macro layer to compensate for patterns that are better captured in the model structure itself.

The two-lag autoregressive term (p=2) was also consistent with domain intuition: this month's deals are correlated with both last month and two months ago, reflecting a typical deal origination-to-submission pipeline that spans 6–8 weeks.

04

Implementation

The backtesting loop and model fitting ran in Python against data pulled via the dbt mart layer. Here's a simplified version of the walk-forward evaluation:

forecast/model_selection.py — walk-forward backtest
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax import SARIMAX
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error, mean_squared_error

# Candidate model orders to evaluate
candidates = [
    {"order": (1,1,1), "seasonal_order": (0,0,0,0)},
    {"order": (2,1,1), "seasonal_order": (0,0,0,0)},
    {"order": (2,1,2), "seasonal_order": (0,0,0,0)},
    {"order": (2,1,1), "seasonal_order": (1,1,0,12)},  # winner
    {"order": (1,1,1), "seasonal_order": (1,1,0,12)},
]

def walk_forward_backtest(series, candidate, n_test=12):
    """Walk-forward evaluation: train up to month N, predict N+1."""
    actuals, predictions = [], []
    train_end = len(series) - n_test

    for i in range(n_test):
        train = series.iloc[:train_end + i]
        actual = series.iloc[train_end + i]

        model = SARIMAX(
            train,
            order=candidate["order"],
            seasonal_order=candidate["seasonal_order"],
            enforce_stationarity=False
        )
        fit = model.fit(disp=False)
        pred = fit.forecast(steps=1).iloc[0]

        actuals.append(actual)
        predictions.append(pred)

    mae  = mean_absolute_error(actuals, predictions)
    rmse = mean_squared_error(actuals, predictions, squared=False)
    dir_acc = sum(
        int((p - actuals[i-1]) * (actuals[i] - actuals[i-1]) > 0)
        for i, p in enumerate(predictions) if i > 0
    ) / (n_test - 1)

    return {"mae": round(mae), "rmse": round(rmse), "dir_acc": round(dir_acc, 2)}

# Run all candidates, rank by MAE
results = [
    {"config": c, **walk_forward_backtest(deal_series, c)}
    for c in candidates
]
best = sorted(results, key=lambda x: x["mae"])[0]

Macro Adjustment Layer

The SARIMA model captures the internal rhythm of deal flow — trend, momentum, and seasonality. But TheGuarantors operates in the rental housing market, and that market is directly shaped by forces outside our historical data: mortgage rates that push renters out of buying, housing supply that constrains or expands our addressable market, and rent growth that affects applicant volumes.

I built an adjustment multiplier from three indicators, each pulled monthly and joined to the forecast output via a SQL model in dbt:

⌂
30-Year Mortgage Rate
Higher mortgage rates suppress homebuying, driving demand into the rental market and increasing TheGuarantors' addressable pool of applicants. Positively correlated with our deal volume with ~2 month lag.
↑
Housing Starts (MoM)
New rental unit supply affects how many landlords need guaranty products. Rising starts signal market expansion; falling starts compress the addressable property base. Used as a 3-month leading indicator.
%
National Rent Growth (YoY)
Rapid rent increases push marginal applicants toward guaranty products. Sourced from Zillow Observed Rent Index and normalized to a 0–1 adjustment weight applied to the forecast output.
models/marts/mart_forecast_macro_adjusted.sql
WITH base_forecast AS (
  SELECT * FROM {{ ref('stg_arima_forecast') }}
  -- output of Python model, loaded via dbt seed
),

macro AS (
  SELECT
    forecast_month,
    -- Normalize each indicator to 0-1 range over trailing 24 months
    (mortgage_rate_30yr - MIN(mortgage_rate_30yr) OVER w)
      / NULLIF(MAX(mortgage_rate_30yr) OVER w - MIN(mortgage_rate_30yr) OVER w, 0)
      AS mortgage_score,

    (housing_starts_mom - MIN(housing_starts_mom) OVER w)
      / NULLIF(MAX(housing_starts_mom) OVER w - MIN(housing_starts_mom) OVER w, 0)
      AS supply_score,

    (rent_growth_yoy - MIN(rent_growth_yoy) OVER w)
      / NULLIF(MAX(rent_growth_yoy) OVER w - MIN(rent_growth_yoy) OVER w, 0)
      AS rent_score

  FROM {{ ref('stg_macro_indicators') }}
  WINDOW w AS (
    ORDER BY forecast_month
    ROWS BETWEEN 23 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW
  )
),

adjusted AS (
  SELECT
    f.forecast_month,
    f.predicted_deals,
    -- Weighted macro multiplier: mortgage 40%, supply 35%, rent 25%
    1.0 + (
      (m.mortgage_score * 0.40)
    + (m.supply_score   * 0.35)
    + (m.rent_score     * 0.25)
    - 0.50          -- center around zero adjustment
    ) * 0.15        -- cap total macro adjustment at ±7.5%
    AS macro_multiplier,

    ROUND(
      f.predicted_deals * (
        1.0 + (
          (m.mortgage_score * 0.40)
        + (m.supply_score   * 0.35)
        + (m.rent_score     * 0.25)
        - 0.50
        ) * 0.15
      )
    ) AS adjusted_deals

  FROM base_forecast f
  LEFT JOIN macro m USING (forecast_month)
)

SELECT * FROM adjusted

The multiplier was deliberately capped at ±7.5% total adjustment. The ARIMA model should do the heavy lifting; the macro layer corrects for known external conditions the historical series can't fully anticipate. Uncapped macro adjustments tend to overfit to short-term indicator movements and reduce forecast stability — exactly the opposite of what planning needs.

08

Lessons Learned

  • 01 Domain knowledge constrains the model search in useful ways. Without knowing that deal volume has an annual lease-cycle pattern, I might have tested non-seasonal models more extensively. Business context cut the candidate space in half and pointed directly at the seasonal differencing that drove the best results.
  • 02 Walk-forward backtesting is the only honest evaluation. In-sample fit metrics are almost always misleading for time series — a model can fit history perfectly and forecast terribly. The walk-forward approach simulates real deployment, which is why it should be the default, not an afterthought.
  • 03 Cap your macro adjustments. Early iterations let the macro multiplier run free, and the resulting forecasts whipsawed with short-term indicator movements. A capped adjustment preserved the model's stability while still incorporating market signals that mattered.
  • 04 The delivery format matters as much as the model. Embedding the forecast output in dbt — so actuals tracked against it automatically every month — transformed it from a one-time deliverable into a living planning instrument. Leadership checked it monthly without any analyst involvement after the initial build.
